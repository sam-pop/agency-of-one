# Process notes: turning artifacts and cards into decisions

## Interpret, don't costume
A drawn medium gives you a **system of logic**, not a texture pack. Take its constraints, not its wallpaper.

| Take from the medium | Don't take |
|---|---|
| Its printing limits (2 inks, no gradients, fixed sizes) | A paper-texture background JPEG |
| Its hierarchy logic (how it tells you what matters first) | Fake torn edges and coffee stains |
| Its grid and information order | Literal props (a clipart ticket, a fake barcode) |
| Its typographic *class* (condensed grotesque, engraved serif, stencil) | A novelty font that imitates the object |
| Its way of handling numbers, codes, and units | Ornament for ornament's sake |

If a stranger would call the result "themed" (a pirate restaurant, a fake-old saloon), you've costumed it. If they'd call it "clearly made by people who know this world," you've interpreted it.

## Reading a medium: the five questions
For each drawn medium, answer these in one line each:
1. **Type:** what classes of letterform did it use, and why (legibility at distance, engraving, stencil cutting, typewriter)?
2. **Ink:** how many colours, what kinds (spot, process, enamel, thermal, fluorescent), and what was forbidden?
3. **Order:** how is information ranked (size, position, boxes, codes, colour-coding)?
4. **Unit:** what's the repeating unit (row, card, tag, cell, slot, entry)?
5. **Mark:** what's the one detail an insider would recognise (punch hole, perforation, registration mark, check digit, stamp)?

Then combine the answers with the subject's §1 artifacts. The subject always wins a conflict; the medium is a lens, not the destination.

## Worked example: a neighbourhood bakery
**Subject artifacts:** string-tied pastry boxes, chalk price board, docket spike, proofing baskets (spiral ridges), flour sacks with stencilled mill names, paper bags with grease marks, the 6am queue, the "sold out" card, oven timers, a bread-number ticket machine.
**Vernacular:** "out of the oven at", "sold out", loaf names, hydration %, bake numbers, Tuesday rye.
**One true thing:** what's coming out of the oven next, and what's already gone.

**Draw:** hardware-store bin labels × theatre playbill · *Navigation IS the hero* · *Single vertical axis at the left third* · signature: *live data element (stock on hand)* · palette: *ultraviolet blacklight and fluorescent pigment*

**Five questions → decisions**
- Bin labels: condensed bold sans for item names, small regular text for codes, colour-coded strips per category, one item per slot, the drawer *is* the index. → Each bread is a labelled "bin" in an index; category by a colour strip, not a card.
- Playbill: one huge name, a strict billing hierarchy, everything else small, tonight's date prominent. → Today's bake is "billed" like a cast list: the loaf out next is top billing.
- Nav-as-hero + left-third axis → the first screen *is* today's board: loaf names hang off a vertical line at 33%, times on the left of the line, names on the right.
- Live stock → each line shows real availability ("12 left", "gone at 9:40") pulled from the POS, with sold-out loaves struck through in the accent colour.
- UV/fluorescent palette → don't go literal neon-on-black (that's a gen-2 tell). Interpret it as the *one fluorescent price-sticker pink* found on real bakery labels, on a mid-tone flour-sack grey-green ground, with dark ink. The fluorescent appears only on "out now" and "sold out" marks.

**Slop twin it beat:** cream ground, Fraunces headline "Baked with love, every morning", terracotta button "Order now →", three cards (Breads · Pastries · Cakes) with photos, eyebrow "OUR STORY".

## Worked example: a B2B lending platform
**Subject artifacts:** term sheets, amortisation schedules, title reports, closing binders with tabs, wire-transfer confirmations, appraisal photos, lien stamps, county recorder seals, Bloomberg-style rate tables.
**Draw:** court docket sheet × paint-store swatch fan deck · *The main content is a real table* · *Maximum three type sizes* · signature: *numbers set in a custom tabular style become the identity* · palette: *postal red and envelope manila*

- The docket gives a ruled, case-numbered ledger structure: each loan program is a "docket entry" with a number, a status, and dates.
- The swatch deck gives the colour-strip logic: each loan product gets one swatch colour, used as an edge strip (never a card fill).
- Three sizes only: one for rates (big), one for text, one for fine print. The rate table *is* the hero: today's rates per product, with real units (bps, LTV %, term in months).
- Signature: oldstyle-free tabular numerals with a slashed zero and a custom per-cent glyph treatment; every figure on the site uses it.
- Palette: manila ground (a real mid-tone, not cream), postal-red accent only on rate changes, ink-blue text.

## When the draw fights the subject
If a card is truly incompatible (fluorescent palette for a hospice), use the single veto with a reason, then redraw. If two cards conflict with each other, resolve the conflict in the subject's favour and say how in DESIGN.md. Tension between cards is usually where the interesting idea is. Don't rush to remove it.

## Copy before layout
Write the hero sentence and the primary action label before drawing wireframes. Layout should serve what the page says, not the reverse. If the hero copy could appear on any competitor's site, rewrite it with §1 vocabulary until it couldn't.
